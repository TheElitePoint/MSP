A practical system for direct Sales Navigator research, reverse engineering, account validation, and buyer mapping

**Purpose:** This manual provides a complete research process for identifying relevant companies, verifying fit, locating the right decision-makers, and preparing accurate records for business development activity.

**Provided as a working research and decision-maker mapping system**

Designed for a B2B operational intelligence platform serving managed technology providers, financial organisations, healthcare IT environments, and operationally complex mid-market companies.

**MODULE 05**

**Understanding the business in simple language**

Before searching for companies or people, understand the operating problem, the solution, and the first commercial step.

The business provides a B2B operational intelligence software platform for organisations that operate or manage complex technology environments. Its purpose is to connect information that normally sits across separate operational systems, then help technology teams understand relationships, consequences, ownership, and the most appropriate next action.

In simple terms, the platform helps an operations team move from knowing that something happened to understanding why it happened, what is affected, who is responsible, and what should happen next. This difference is important because most organisations already have tools that create alerts, tickets, asset records, change records, and reports. The missing layer is often connected operational understanding.

**Business model at a glance**

| **Business characteristic**       | **What it means for research**                                                                                                     |
| --------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Business type**                 | B2B enterprise software for technology operations, service delivery, infrastructure, resilience, and operational governance.       |
| **Primary customer environments** | Organisations managing many systems, services, assets, users, customers, changes, incidents, and operational dependencies.         |
| **Typical users**                 | IT operations, infrastructure, incident, change, service delivery, configuration, architecture, risk, and security teams.          |
| **Typical buyers**                | Technology executives, operations leaders, service-delivery leaders, and business owners in smaller managed-service companies.     |
| **First commercial step**         | A focused 30-to-60-day evaluation or pilot around one or two operational use cases, followed by a possible recurring subscription. |

**The four questions the platform is designed to answer**

**•** What happened across the environment?

**•** Why did it happen, and what recent event or dependency may be connected?

**•** What services, customers, teams, or business operations are affected?

**•** Who owns the affected area, and what action should be considered next?

**Research implication:** Do not search only for companies that use a particular industry label. Search for organisations where technology complexity, service responsibility, and fragmented operational information create a genuine need for connected understanding.

**MODULE 06**

**Why operational teams struggle**

The market problem is not usually a lack of tools. It is the absence of one reliable operational context across those tools.

Modern technology environments are built from cloud services, applications, databases, networks, identity systems, endpoints, security tools, integrations, vendors, and internal teams. A single customer-facing service may depend on many technical components owned by different people. When one component changes or fails, the impact can travel across several services before the true cause is understood.

Most operational tools are useful, but each one normally answers a narrow question. Monitoring may show that a resource is unhealthy. A ticketing system may record the incident. An asset system may list a device. A change system may show what was deployed. A knowledge base may contain a recovery procedure. The difficulty is connecting these records quickly and accurately during a live operational event.

**What existing systems show, and what may still be missing**

| **Existing system**               | **What it usually does well**                                | **Context that may remain unclear**                                                           |
| --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Monitoring and observability      | Detects alerts, performance changes, and technical symptoms. | Which business service is affected, which dependency matters most, and who owns the response. |
| Ticketing and incident management | Records issues, assignments, status, and communication.      | Whether several tickets share one underlying cause or relate to a recent change.              |
| Asset and configuration records   | Lists assets, systems, owners, or configuration items.       | Whether relationships are current and how impact may spread through connected services.       |
| Change management                 | Records planned and completed changes.                       | The full downstream risk of a change across services, customers, and operational teams.       |
| Knowledge and runbooks            | Stores instructions and previous resolution knowledge.       | Which guidance is relevant to the current dependency, incident, and business context.         |

**A common operational scenario**

A customer portal begins failing. Monitoring creates several alerts. The service desk opens incidents. An authentication service appears unhealthy, but a network change was also completed earlier that day. The application, authentication service, database, network component, and customer-support process are owned by different teams. Leadership wants to know whether customers are affected and how long recovery may take.

Without connected context, teams may investigate symptoms independently, repeat work, escalate to the wrong owners, or rely on individual memory. The platform is intended to bring service relationships, incidents, changes, risks, ownership, and relevant knowledge into one operational view so the response can begin from a shared understanding.

**Research implication:** Strong prospects are not simply companies with many tools. They are companies where several tools, teams, customers, and services must be coordinated under time pressure.

**MODULE 07**

**The two-layer solution**

The platform combines a connected operational record with an AI-assisted understanding layer.

The solution can be understood as two complementary layers. The first creates and maintains the connected operational picture. The second analyses that picture to explain likely causes, affected services, risk, and possible next actions. The value comes from using both layers together.

| **Layer**                           | **Primary role**                                                              | **Typical information**                                                                                                            | **Typical outputs**                                                                                                                                |
| ----------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Connected operational record**    | Creates a structured view of the environment and the relationships within it. | Assets, configuration items, services, applications, dependencies, incidents, changes, risks, knowledge, ownership, and workflows. | Topology views, service maps, ownership records, operational dashboards, incident and change context, and executive reporting.                     |
| **Operational understanding layer** | Analyses connected information and assists human decision-making.             | Current and historical events, dependencies, changes, risk records, ownership, operational knowledge, and service relationships.   | Impact analysis, likely-cause guidance, dependency-aware recommendations, change-risk assessments, operational summaries, and executive briefings. |

**How information moves through the platform**

| **1\. Operational data**                                                                | **2\. Connected model**                                                      | **3\. Contextual analysis**                                                          | **4\. Human decision**                                                   | **5\. Business outcome**                                                                        |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Systems, services, assets, alerts, incidents, changes, risks, ownership, and knowledge. | Relationships and dependencies are organised into a shared operational view. | The intelligence layer examines cause, impact, risk, history, and relevant guidance. | The responsible team evaluates the evidence and selects the next action. | Faster understanding, clearer accountability, reduced operational risk, and stronger reporting. |

**What makes the combination valuable**

**•** A record without relationships may become another database that teams must maintain.

**•** An AI assistant without reliable operational context may produce generic or unsupported recommendations.

**•** A connected record plus contextual analysis can help teams understand technical events in operational and business terms.

**•** Executive outputs can translate technical complexity into affected services, business risk, ownership, and recovery status.

**Important boundary:** The platform supports analysis and informed action. It should not be described as automatically controlling a customer environment or guaranteeing that every recommendation is correct.

**MODULE 10**

**The initial commercial model and pilot logic**

The first objective is a contained operational evaluation, not a complete enterprise transformation.

The platform is introduced through a focused 30-to-60-day pilot or design-partner engagement. The purpose is to prove value on one important operational problem while limiting customer adoption risk. Research should therefore identify organisations that can provide a controlled environment, a responsible operational owner, suitable data, and enough urgency to evaluate the solution within a practical timeframe.

**Typical pilot sequence**

| **Pilot stage**                   | **Customer and platform activity**                                                                               | **Evidence created**                                                                                |
| --------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **1\. Discovery and scoping**     | Map the operating environment, current tools, priority services, pain, owners, and one or two use cases.         | Agreed problem statement, participants, data requirements, scope, and success measures.             |
| **2\. Limited environment setup** | Use a sandbox, selected service, controlled environment, or limited data set rather than the full enterprise.    | A practical deployment path and confirmation that the necessary information can be connected.       |
| **3\. Use-case validation**       | Test dependency mapping, incident impact, change visibility, ownership, guided recovery, or executive reporting. | Observable operational insight and customer feedback on accuracy, usability, and value.             |
| **4\. Weekly working sessions**   | Review findings, gaps, adoption barriers, questions, and possible expansion areas with the operational team.     | Documented learning, objections, user language, and internal use cases.                             |
| **5\. End-of-pilot decision**     | Summarise value created, risks surfaced, unresolved gaps, and the path to a larger deployment or subscription.   | A commercial next step, expansion plan, referenceable learning, or a clear decision not to proceed. |

**What a pilot-ready prospect looks like**

**•** A real operational problem connected to incidents, dependencies, changes, service ownership, resilience, or executive understanding.

**•** A defined operational owner who can participate in discovery and weekly review.

**•** A limited environment or use case that can be evaluated without replacing the entire technology stack.

**•** Reasonable access to data, integrations, imports, or a sandbox environment.

**•** A decision path that allows evaluation or pilot approval within approximately 30 to 60 days.

**•** Potential to expand into a subscription, wider deployment, or multiple managed customer environments if value is proven.

**Research implication:** Prioritise companies with both fit and the ability to act. A company can have strong pain but still be a weak first target if procurement, access, ownership, or pilot readiness is unclear.

**Strong company signals**

| -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |

**Mandatory questions before saving an account**

**•** What does the company actually provide or operate?

**•** What evidence shows ongoing responsibility for technology services, customers, or critical systems?

**•** Which operational problem is most likely to be relevant?

**•** Which use case could be evaluated in a limited pilot?

**•** Who owns the problem, who evaluates technology, and who controls the budget?

**•** Is there a reason to prioritise the account now?

**•** Which facts are verified, and which points are only research hypotheses?

**•** What evidence should be written in the tracker so another person can reproduce the decision?

**Do not assume:** an integration is available, a specific tool is installed, the company already wants a solution, the platform replaces all existing systems, or a public post proves buying intent. Record hypotheses as hypotheses and verify material claims before use.

**Internal positioning sentence:** The platform connects operational records and service relationships, then helps technology teams understand what happened, why it happened, what is affected, who owns the response, and what action should be considered next.

**MODULE 14**

**Research principles**

These rules protect quality while leaving room for intelligent discovery.

| **Principle**            | **Meaning**                                                                                            |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Account first            | Understand the company before selecting people. A senior title at the wrong company is not useful.     |
| Evidence over assumption | Use public evidence to support fit. Do not infer customers, tools, revenue, or maturity without proof. |
| Problem before product   | Search for the operating problem and environment, not only the product category.                       |
| Several smaller searches | Use focused queries for each service model, vertical, geography, or buyer type.                        |
| Triangulation            | Important accounts should be supported by more than one source or signal.                              |
| Clear exclusions         | Removing poor-fit accounts is part of good research, not lost output.                                  |
| Learning loop            | Record useful keywords, sources, and patterns discovered during the work.                              |
| Human review             | AI can accelerate analysis, but final records must be verified manually.                               |

**Quality test:** Would a colleague understand why this company matters, who should be contacted, and what evidence supports the conclusion?

**MODULE 15**

**Researcher judgement and autonomy**

The system defines standards, but the researcher is expected to think beyond the obvious search path.

Not every relevant company will describe itself using the same industry labels. Some high-quality managed technology providers may call themselves cloud partners, technology advisors, infrastructure specialists, digital workplace providers, security operators, or co-managed IT firms. Good research identifies the operating model behind the language.

**Where judgement is required**

• Deciding whether the company truly manages ongoing client environments or only sells projects

• Recognising when a smaller company has enough complexity and buying capacity

• Distinguishing operational buyers from impressive but irrelevant titles

• Testing new search phrases when standard keywords produce weak results

• Choosing when one strong source is enough and when more evidence is necessary

• Identifying adjacent segments that share the same operational problem

• Explaining why a recent event creates a useful timing signal

**A useful research habit**

At the end of each research session, write down one observation: a repeated phrase, an unexpected buyer title, a useful directory, a common exclusion, or a new hypothesis. Over time, these observations become a better research system than any static list of filters.

**Permission to explore:** New search routes are encouraged when they remain relevant, evidence-based, and properly documented.

**Signal logic:** The best accounts usually show several operating conditions at the same time, not only one broad industry label.

**MODULE 19**

**Mapping rule:** Do not select people only because they are senior. Select them because their responsibilities connect to the problem, evaluation, or commercial decision.

**MODULE 23**

**Fast-follow profile: Regulated mid-market IT**

Use this segment when the operating problem is clear but the company does not fit the first three labels.

Regulated or operationally sensitive mid-market companies can be attractive because they combine meaningful infrastructure complexity with faster access than very large enterprises. **Qualification questions**

**Useful titles**

VP of IT Operations, Director of Infrastructure, Head of Technology Operations, Director of Service Management, Head of Incident and Change, Enterprise Architecture leader, Operational Resilience leader, or CIO and CTO in smaller companies.

**MODULE 24**

**Geographic strategy**

Use geography to improve access, relevance, and execution, not only to reduce search volume.

| **Geographic factor**   | **Research use**                                                                     |

**3\.** Use local directories, awards, associations, and event lists.

**4\.** Check whether the account serves customers beyond its headquarters location.

**5\.** Record both company headquarters and the decision-maker location.

**Do not over-filter:** A strong account should not be rejected only because the relevant decision-maker works remotely or the company operates across several regions.

**MODULE 26**

**Account qualification rules**

A company should pass the minimum standard before decision-maker mapping begins.

| **Qualification area** | **Minimum question**                                                                                                              |
| ---------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Business model         | Does the company genuinely operate or manage technology, rather than only advise, recruit, resell, or complete one-time projects? |
| Operational complexity | Are there multiple services, systems, customer environments, dependencies, or operating teams?                                    |
| Problem relevance      | Is there a credible need for incident, change, dependency, ownership, risk, or executive visibility?                              |
| Commercial ability     | Is the organisation likely to fund and support a focused pilot?                                                                   |
| Buyer access           | Can at least one relevant decision-maker be identified?                                                                           |
| Timing or priority     | Is there a reason this account may be receptive now, or is it a strong evergreen fit?                                             |
| Evidence               | Can the conclusion be supported by reliable public sources?                                                                       |

**Minimum acceptance rule**

**Approved account:** The company must pass business-model fit, operational relevance, and buyer access. Strong Tier A accounts should also show timing or unusually high problem intensity.

**When to hold for review**

Use a review status when the company appears relevant but important facts remain unclear, such as whether services are recurring, whether the company has operational responsibility, or whether the right buyer can be located. Do not force uncertain records into the approved list.

**MODULE 27**

**Strong-fit signals**

The strongest accounts show a combination of structural fit, operational need, and timing.

| **Signal group**           | **Examples**                                                                                              |
| -------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Structural fit             | Recurring managed services, several client environments, multiple systems or locations, mid-market scale  |
| Operational responsibility | 24/7 support, SLAs, NOC, SOC, service desk, incident response, business continuity                        |
| Problem intensity          | Tool fragmentation, complex dependencies, high change activity, customer escalations, risk reporting      |
| Buyer presence             | Visible operations, service-delivery, infrastructure, technology, resilience, or executive leaders        |
| Growth and change          | Hiring, acquisition, new service, new region, cloud migration, platform transformation                    |
| Vertical pressure          | Healthcare, financial services, regulated customers, critical infrastructure, high-trust services         |
| Public intent              | Leaders discussing operational efficiency, resilience, incidents, automation, service quality, or scaling |

**Strong account pattern**

A high-priority managed technology provider may have 80 employees, a 24/7 operations centre, healthcare customers, several cloud partnerships, and an open role for a service-delivery manager. No single fact proves fit, but the combined pattern creates a strong case.

**Research note:** Record the pattern in one sentence. Do not paste a list of unrelated facts without explaining what they mean.

**MODULE 28**

**Weak-fit and exclusion signals**

Protect research capacity by identifying unsuitable accounts early.

| **Weak or excluded type**                      | **Reason**                                                                       |
| ---------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| One-person IT freelancer                       | Insufficient operational complexity and buying capacity                          |
| Recruitment or staffing company                | May use MSP language but does not manage technology operations                   |
| Web design or digital agency                   | Project delivery is not the target operating model                               |
| Hardware retailer or reseller only             | No ongoing responsibility for customer environments                              |
| Telecom broker only                            | Limited operational use case unless it also provides managed technology services |
| Residential computer support                   | Wrong buyer and commercial model                                                 |
| Consultancy without managed operations         | Advisory work alone does not create the required operating pain                  |
| Very large enterprise without timing or access | Long cycle and low practicality for the first pilot stage                        |
| Company with unclear or inactive presence      | Insufficient evidence or possible outdated record                                |

**Common false positive**

A company may include "managed services" in a broad capabilities list but generate most of its business from projects, staffing, software development, or equipment sales. Review the service pages, case studies, careers, and leadership before accepting it.

**Exclusion discipline:** Document the exclusion reason. This prevents repeated research on the same unsuitable company and improves future search filters.

**MODULE 29**

**Evidence standards**

Every important conclusion should be traceable to a reliable source.

| **Evidence level**          | **Examples**                                                                                                  | **Use**                        |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Level 1: Direct             | Company website, official company page, leadership profile, job posting, public case study, regulatory filing | Use as primary validation      |
| Level 2: Strong external    | Recognised association, partner directory, event agenda, reputable business database, customer announcement   | Use to support or discover     |
| Level 3: Discovery only     | Search snippets, AI output, generic list articles, unverified directories, copied profile summaries           | Use to find leads, then verify |
| Level 4: Unacceptable alone | Assumption, memory, unclear aggregator, outdated cached text, anonymous claim                                 | Do not use as final evidence   |

**Evidence recording standard**

• Save the source link, not only the source name.

• Write the specific fact supported by the source.

• Record the date checked when the information may change.

• Avoid claiming a technology, customer, revenue figure, or partnership unless clearly supported.

• When sources conflict, note the conflict and use the most direct current source.

**AI rule:** AI summaries are research assistance, not proof. Verify material facts through direct or strong external sources.

**MODULE 30**

**Account scoring model**

Use scoring to create consistency, then apply judgement to the final priority.

| **Category**          | **Weight** | **What to score**                                                                    |
| --------------------- | ---------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Ideal company fit     | 40         | Business model, size, geography, customer type, operational scale                    |
| Operational need      | 25         | Incidents, dependencies, change, ownership, resilience, fragmented tools             |
| Timing signals        | 20         | Hiring, acquisition, expansion, transformation, leadership change, public intent     |

| **Score** | **Tier** | **Action**                                                         |
| --------- | -------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| 75 to 100 | Tier A   | Immediate completion, decision-maker mapping, and priority handoff |

**MODULE 31**

**Priority tiers and work order**

The tier determines the depth and speed of research.

| **Tier** | **Research depth**                                          | **Decision-maker target**   | **Expected rationale**                            |
| -------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| A        | Full website, LinkedIn, signals, evidence, and source trail | Two to four people          | Specific fit, need, timing, and buyer logic       |
| B        | Full fit validation with lighter timing research            | Two to three people         | Clear fit and problem relevance                   |
| C        | Basic validation and reason for holding                     | One or two people if useful | Possible fit but weak timing, access, or evidence |
| Exclude  | Record only enough to prevent duplicate review              | None                        | Clear reason for exclusion                        |

**Recommended work order**

**1\.** Build a broad raw account universe.

**2\.** Quick-screen obvious exclusions.

**3\.** Fully validate the strongest possible Tier A accounts.

**4\.** Map decision-makers for approved Tier A accounts.

**5\.** Complete Tier B accounts using the same standards.

**6\.** Use Tier C accounts only when the stronger pool is insufficient or when they reveal useful market patterns.

**Capacity rule:** Do not spend equal time on every account. Invest deeper research where the expected commercial value and relevance are higher.

**MODULE 32**

**Direct Sales Navigator research: Overview**

Use structured filters to create coverage, then validate each result outside the search page.

Direct research begins with an account search. The purpose is to generate a manageable universe of companies that match the assignment by geography, industry, size, service language, and business model. Only after accounts are reviewed and saved should the researcher run lead searches within those approved companies.

| **Step** | **Action**                                                           |
| -------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1        | Create several focused account searches rather than one broad search |
| 2        | Apply geography, industry, size, and keyword filters                 |
| 3        | Open company pages and websites to verify the operating model        |
| 4        | Save approved accounts into named lists by segment and tier          |
| 5        | Run lead searches inside the saved account list                      |
| 6        | Review titles, responsibilities, activity, and relevance             |
| 7        | Complete the tracker with evidence and a clear rationale             |

**Why account-first research matters**

A people-first search can produce consultants, vendors, employees at unsuitable companies, and titles using the same keywords for unrelated responsibilities. Account-first research keeps the company model consistent and makes decision-maker mapping more accurate.

**Platform note:** Filter names and availability may vary by subscription. Use the closest available option and document any limitations.

**MODULE 34**

**Account filters**

Use filters as a starting point, then rely on manual verification.

| **Filter**        | **Recommended use**                                                                                              |
| ----------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Geography         | Primary country first, then focused regional searches                                                            |

• Change one major variable at a time so you can understand why results improve or worsen.

• Do not use too many negative keywords until you know what relevant accounts may be removed.

• Separate different service models instead of mixing every keyword into one search.

• Record which filters produced the strongest qualified-account rate.

**MODULE 35**

**Keyword and Boolean rules**

Use search logic to improve relevance without making the query impossible to understand.

| **Operator or technique** | **Use**                                                                           |
| ------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Quotation marks           | Search an exact phrase such as "managed IT services"                              |
| OR                        | Include alternative phrases such as "managed service provider" OR "co-managed IT" |
| AND                       | Require two concepts such as managed services AND healthcare                      |
| NOT                       | Remove repeated false positives only after reviewing results                      |
| Parentheses               | Group alternatives before combining them with another concept                     |
| Short query sets          | Use several clear queries rather than one extremely long string                   |

**Good query pattern**

**Structure:** (service phrase A OR service phrase B) AND (vertical or operating signal)

**Practical rules**

• Write operators in uppercase.

• Use exact phrases for multi-word services or titles.

• Test the simple version before adding exclusions.

• Review the first 20 to 30 results and note why weak accounts appear.

• Create a revised query based on evidence, not guesswork.

**Avoid:** Searching only the abbreviation MSP. It has unrelated meanings and usually produces a noisy result set.

| Title is senior but responsibilities are unrelated                                          | Do not select only for seniority    |

**Profile review checklist**

\[ \] Current title and company match the record

\[ \] Responsibilities connect to the problem or decision

\[ \] The person has enough seniority for the expected role

\[ \] Recent activity, tenure, and background support relevance

\[ \] The profile is not a duplicate, former employee, consultant, or unrelated adviser

| **Check**           | **Standard**                                                                          |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |

**MODULE 45**

**Direct research worked example**

A model of how a raw search result becomes a qualified account.

| **Research step**    | **Example finding**                                                                                              |
| -------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |

**MODULE 46**

**Reverse-engineering research: Overview**

Start from strong evidence and work backward to discover similar companies and buyers.

Reverse engineering is the process of identifying high-quality examples, analysing the characteristics that make them relevant, and using those characteristics to discover additional accounts through ecosystems, public signals, relationships, and repeated language. It is especially useful when companies do not use standard industry labels or when direct searches produce too many weak results.

| **Input**                | **Reverse-engineering question**                                          |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Strong seed company      | Which companies look and operate like this one?                           |
| Technology vendor        | Which service providers use, sell, implement, or appear in its ecosystem? |
| Event or association     | Which companies and leaders repeatedly appear in this community?          |
| Case study               | Which companies have the same customer profile or operating problem?      |
| Job posting              | Which companies are building the same operational capabilities?           |
| Acquisition or expansion | Which organisations are becoming more operationally complex?              |
| Leader activity          | Which peers engage with the same problems and industry conversations?     |

**Core rule:** Reverse engineering improves discovery. Every company must still pass the same qualification, scoring, and evidence standards.

**MODULE 47**

**MODULE 53**

**Case-study and customer-proof method**

Customer stories reveal operating models, technology environments, and similar-company clusters.

**1\.** Find case studies published by relevant vendors, cloud platforms, software tools, and service providers.

**2\.** Identify the customer, provider, technology, business problem, and operational result.

**3\.** Review the named provider to determine whether it fits the target model.

**4\.** Search for other providers serving the same customer industry or solving the same problem.

**5\.** Use distinctive phrases from the case study in web and LinkedIn searches.

**6\.** Record the case study as a discovery source and verify current company details separately.

**What case studies can reveal**

• Real customer industries and account size

• Service responsibility and delivery model

• Technology stack and integration complexity

• Operational pain such as downtime, visibility, change, or reporting

• Decision-maker titles involved in the project

• Peer companies likely to face the same problem

**Do not overclaim:** A public case study shows one project or relationship. It does not automatically prove recurring revenue, company-wide adoption, or current buying intent.

**MODULE 55**

**Acquisition, expansion, and transformation signals**

| **Signal**               | **Possible operational implication**                                       |
| ------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Acquisition or merger    | Duplicate tools, combined teams, unclear ownership, service integration    |
| New office or region     | More infrastructure, users, networks, and support requirements             |
| New managed-service line | New workflows, skills, customer commitments, and operational reporting     |
| Cloud migration          | Changing dependencies, hybrid systems, incident and change risk            |
| Platform modernisation   | Integration, service mapping, ownership, and adoption challenges           |
| Rapid customer growth    | Higher ticket volume, service-delivery pressure, and escalation complexity |

**Research process**

**1\.** Confirm the event through a direct or strong external source.

**2\.** Identify which systems, teams, services, or customers are likely affected.

**3\.** Find the leaders responsible for integration, operations, infrastructure, or service delivery.

**4\.** Use the event as a prioritisation signal, not as an unsupported outreach claim.

**5\.** Record the date and source because timing information changes.

**MODULE 59**

**AI-assisted research workflow**

Use AI to expand thinking, organise evidence, and identify gaps. Verify the final record manually.

| **AI use**        | **Good application**                                                                   | **Required human check**                            |
| ----------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Keyword expansion | Generate alternative industry, service, and buyer language                             | Test terms in real searches and remove weak phrases |
| Company analysis  | Summarise business model, services, customers, and possible fit from supplied evidence | Verify facts on company sources                     |
| Comparison        | Compare a candidate with the ideal company profile                                     | Review assumptions and missing evidence             |
| Title discovery   | Suggest likely buyer roles by company size and segment                                 | Confirm actual roles on profiles                    |

**Safe operating method**

**1\.** Provide AI with the assignment criteria and the evidence already collected.

**2\.** Ask it to distinguish facts, inferences, unknowns, and questions.

**3\.** Use its output to guide additional research.

**4\.** Verify important facts through direct sources.

**5\.** Write the final tracker record in clear human language.

**MODULE 60**

**AI prompt library: Discovery and analysis**

Adapt these prompts with the exact assignment and evidence.

**Keyword expansion**

**Prompt:** We are researching \[company type\] in \[geography\]. The ideal company has \[operating characteristics\]. Generate grouped search phrases for direct LinkedIn account searches, web searches, partner directories, jobs, events, and case studies. Explain what each phrase is expected to find.

**MODULE 61**

**AI prompt library: Decision-makers and quality control**

Use AI to challenge the work, not to replace verification.

**Decision-maker map**

**Prompt:** For a \[size\] company in \[segment\], classify the following people as economic buyer, technical buyer, operational buyer, champion, risk influencer, or low relevance. Explain the classification using title and responsibilities only. People: \[paste names, titles, and profile evidence\].

**Research-gap check**

**Prompt:** Review this account record against these required fields: business model, services, company size, geography, customer industries, operational signals, timing, decision-makers, sources, fit reason, score, and status. List missing or weak fields and the exact research action needed for each.

**False-positive test**

**Prompt:** Challenge the assumption that this company is a good target. Give the strongest reasons it may be unsuitable, the evidence needed to resolve each concern, and a final status of approve, hold, or exclude based only on the supplied information.

**Weekly pattern review**

**Prompt:** Analyse these approved and excluded account records. Identify repeated strong-fit signals, common exclusion reasons, productive discovery sources, title patterns, and search terms that should be added, removed, or tested next week.
